\section{Data Collection and Preprocessing}
\subsection{Geospatial Data Collection and Aggregation}

The project's foundation is built on a comprehensive multi-layered geographic dataset covering the commercial areas in Kathmandu and Bhaktapur. The data collection process evolved through two iterations to achieve the richness and scale required by the HeteroGNN:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Manual Data Collection (Initial Phase):} Initially, data was collected manually through field surveys and Google Maps exploration. While this produced a clean seed dataset, the approach proved highly time-consuming and impossible to scale across the full study area.
	
	\item \textbf{Open-Source Google Maps Scraper (Primary Tool):} To overcome the limitations of manual collection, the team adopted the open-source \textbf{\texttt{google\allowbreak-\allowbreak maps\allowbreak-\allowbreak scraper}} command-line tool, a self-hosted, headless scraper built on top of Playwright that directly queries Google Maps at scale. Unlike browser extensions or third-party SaaS platforms, this tool runs locally and returns significantly richer per-business records, including:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Individual user reviews} (text and rating) enabling review-count and sentiment signals.
		\item \textbf{Foot traffic / popular-times data} providing temporal demand indicators.
		\item Full business metadata: category, address, coordinates, overall rating, phone, website, opening hours, and price level.
	\end{itemize}
	This tool enabled the collection of a comprehensive dataset of approximately \textbf{40,000 total data entries} across all categories, covering Points of Interest (POI) and competitor businesses across the study area. Among these, the cafe/competitor category alone contributed approximately \textbf{10,000 entries}, while POI records (banks, education, health, temples, and other amenities) accounted for approximately \textbf{30,000 entries}.
\end{enumerate}

The final curated layers used by the system are organized by category:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Competitors (Cafes):} Existing cafe and restaurant locations scraped with \texttt{google\allowbreak-\allowbreak maps\allowbreak-\allowbreak scraper} and cleaned into a standardized schema, then stored as structured records in the \textbf{SpatiaLite database} (\texttt{competitors} table) with spatial indexing for fast proximity queries.
    \item \textbf{Points of Interest (POIs):} Foot-traffic attractors grouped into categories including \textbf{banks, education, health, temples, and other amenities}, scraped using the same tool and stored in the \textbf{SpatiaLite database} (\texttt{points\allowbreak\_\allowbreak of\allowbreak\_\allowbreak interest} table). The richer scraped attributes (ratings, review counts, foot-traffic signals) are used as node feature inputs in the HeteroGNN graph.
    \item \textbf{Road Network Graph (context feature):} A serialized OSMnx road graph (\texttt{road\_graph.pkl}) encodes the full directed road network, enabling the HeteroGNN to compute road-network-aware shortest-path distances and use road topology as contextual edge features during graph construction.

\end{itemize}

\noindent Overall, the preprocessing pipeline ingests scraped raw data into a \textbf{SpatiaLite spatial database} for fast query access, while the road network is stored as a serialized OSMnx graph pickle. Both data assets are consumed by the backend FastAPI services and the React/Vite-based frontend map interface.
\subsection{Ground Truth Score and Feature Engineering}

A crucial preprocessing stage involves establishing a ``ground truth'' metric of success for existing competitor cafes and preparing the feature vectors for the graph nodes. Unlike earlier iterations that manually flattened spatial features into tabular rows, the current pipeline cleanly separates a location's intrinsic success (the target variable) from its spatial context (left for the graph to model).

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ground Truth Composite Score (Target Variable $\mathbf{y}$):} For each existing cafe, a continuous target variable is constructed to represent its commercial success and popularity. This composite score is derived exclusively from the cafe's own scraped metrics:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Review Rating and Distribution:} The overall star rating and the distribution of reviews.
        \item \textbf{Review Volume:} The total number of reviews, acting as a strong proxy for historical customer footfall.
        \item \textbf{Sentiment Analysis:} Natural Language Processing (NLP) is applied to the scraped \texttt{user\_reviews} text to extract sentiment scores, providing a qualitative measure of customer satisfaction beyond raw star ratings.
        \item \textbf{Popular Times (Foot Traffic):} Temporal demand indicators and peak-hour foot-traffic proxies.
    \end{itemize}
    These intrinsic signals are combined via a weighted sum, passed through outlier handling (clipping extremes, log-scaling), and normalized to a 0--100 scale. \textbf{Importantly, no road network distance or surrounding POI information is included in this composite score.} Spatial relationships are intentionally omitted from the target variable so the HeteroGNN can organically explore and learn how spatial topology and road networks influence this score.

    \item \textbf{Node Feature Engineering (Inputs $\mathbf{X}$):} Both candidate locations and surrounding POIs require rich feature vectors. For POIs, missing metadata is handled gracefully so POIs without certain fields can still be embedded. Scraped attributes (like POI category, rating, review count, and sentiment where applicable) are normalized and attached directly to the nodes in the graph. 
    
    \item \textbf{Spatial Edges and Context (Baseline vs. GNN):} For the baseline XGBoost model, the pipeline computes static distance-aware aggregations (counts and weighted sums with distance-decay) within an analysis radius. However, for the primary HeteroGNN model, spatial relationships are modeled dynamically as edges. The road network graph (\texttt{road\_graph.pkl}) provides the topological context, allowing the model to compute actual routing distances between candidate sites, competitors, and POIs during message passing.
\end{enumerate}

The output of this preprocessing phase is a clean SpatiaLite database table where each cafe possesses a refined, normalized \texttt{composite\_score} alongside its intrinsic metadata. This data is subsequently queried during the graph construction phase to dynamically build HeteroData objects containing node features, edge indices, and the regression labels.

\subsection{Graph Construction Methodology Overview}
As part of the shift towards the Heterogeneous Graph Neural Network (HeteroGNN) architecture in the 8th semester, the data representation paradigm fundamentally changed. Instead of flattening spatial features into tabular rows (as done for XGBoost), the system constructs a \textbf{Heterogeneous Graph}. In this graph:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nodes} represent the diverse physical entities in the urban environment, such as candidate locations, existing competitor cafes, and various Points of Interest (POIs) like banks or health facilities. Each node type carries its own distinct set of features (e.g., POI categories, competitor ratings).
    \item \textbf{Edges} represent the spatial and contextual relationships between these nodes. For instance, an edge might encode the road-network distance between a candidate site and a nearby POI, or the competitive pressure from a nearby existing cafe.
\end{itemize}
This graph structure is dynamically generated for any user-selected location and passed into the PyTorch Geometric-based HeteroGNN model, allowing the system to learn multi-hop dependencies and attention weights directly from the topology of the area. More detailed mathematical formulations of this process are presented below.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulas for Graph Construction and Learning}

This section presents the primary mathematical formulation used by the production model. Hand-crafted distance-decay features are retained as optional baseline features, but the main spatial influence is learned through heterogeneous graph message passing.

\subsubsection{Heterogeneous graph definition}
Let the data be represented as a typed graph:
\begin{equation}
G=(V,E,\mathcal{T}_V,\mathcal{T}_E)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_V$ and $\mathcal{T}_E$ are node and edge type sets. In SiteX, the node types are $\mathcal{T}_V = \{\texttt{place},\, \texttt{category},\, \texttt{road\_node}\}$; edge types encode typed spatial and semantic relations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(\texttt{place},\, \texttt{has\_category},\, \texttt{category})$ and its reverse
    \item $(\texttt{place},\, \texttt{near},\, \texttt{road\_node})$ and its reverse
    \item $(\texttt{road\_node},\, \texttt{connected\_to},\, \texttt{road\_node})$ and its reverse
\end{itemize}

Each node $i$ starts with a type-specific feature vector:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i^{(0)} = \phi_{\tau(i)}(\mathbf{x}_i)
\end{equation}
where $\tau(i)\in\mathcal{T}_V$ is the node type and $\phi_{\tau(i)}$ is a type-specific projection into a common latent space.

\subsubsection{Relation-aware mean aggregation (HeteroGNN / SAGEConv)}
For each relation type $r\in\mathcal{T}_E$, GraphSAGE-style mean aggregation is applied. The neighborhood mean is computed and then combined with the node's own embedding:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_{\mathcal{N}_r(i)}^{(\ell)} = \text{MEAN}\left(\left\{\mathbf{W}_r^{(\text{neigh})}\,\mathbf{h}_j^{(\ell)} \mid j\in\mathcal{N}_r(i)\right\}\right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1,r)} = \mathbf{W}_r^{(\text{self})}\,\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)} + \mathbf{m}_{\mathcal{N}_r(i)}^{(\ell)}
\end{equation}

For nodes with multiple incoming relation types, the \texttt{HeteroConv} wrapper aggregates contributions using summation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1)} = \text{ELU}\!\left(\sum_{r\in\mathcal{T}_E : i\in\text{dst}(r)} \mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1,r)}\right)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Regression head and objective}
After $L$ message-passing layers, the candidate node embedding is passed through a regression head:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i = f_{\text{reg}}\!\left(\mathbf{h}^{(L)}_i\right)
\end{equation}
Training minimizes mean squared error over target nodes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\hat{y}_i - y_i\right)^2
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Ground Truth Score Formulation (Target Variable)}
Unlike earlier iterations that flattened spatial distance-decays into the target score, the HeteroGNN pipeline uses a purely intrinsic composite score as the ground truth target $y_i$ for each cafe node $i$. This isolates the spatial learning entirely to the graph message-passing layers.

The raw composite score is constructed as a weighted sum of the scraped success metrics:
\begin{equation}
S_i = w_1 \cdot \text{rating}_i + w_2 \cdot \log(1 + \text{count}_i) + w_3 \cdot \text{sentiment}_i + w_4 \cdot \text{traffic}_i
\end{equation}
where $w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4$ are the respective importance weights for the star rating, review volume, NLP sentiment score, and popular times (foot traffic) metrics. The review count is log-scaled to dampen the effect of extreme viral outliers.

The final target variable $y_i$ is then normalized to a 0--100 scale:
\begin{equation}
y_i = 100 \times \frac{S_i - \min(S)}{\max(S) - \min(S)}
\end{equation}
This normalized continuous value is used in the Mean Squared Error (MSE) objective function during HeteroGNN training.

\section{Database Schema and Storage}

To support real-time feature calculation and the overall application logic, all aggregated geographic data is stored in a \textbf{SpatiaLite} spatial database---an SQLite extension with native geometry column support and R-Tree spatial indexing. In addition, the road network is persisted as a serialized OSMnx graph pickle for fast path computation.

\subsection{Why SpatiaLite?}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Spatial indexing:} Built-in R-Tree index on geometry columns enables sub-millisecond bounding-box and nearest-neighbor lookups during graph construction and map queries.
	\item \textbf{Single-file portability:} The entire spatial database is contained in a single \texttt{.db} file, simplifying deployment and version control.
	\item \textbf{SQL + geometry:} Standard SQL queries can be combined with spatial functions (e.g., \texttt{ST\_Distance}, \texttt{ST\_Within}) without an external database server.
	\item \textbf{FastAPI integration:} The backend (\textbf{FastAPI} + \textbf{aiosqlite}/\textbf{SQLAlchemy}) queries SpatiaLite asynchronously, replacing the previous in-memory GeoPandas/BallTree approach with a persistent, indexed store.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Database Schema}
The SpatiaLite database is organized into four primary tables:
\begin{description}

    \item[\texttt{competitors}] Existing cafe and competitor business records scraped via the \texttt{google\allowbreak-\allowbreak maps\allowbreak-\allowbreak scraper} tool. Columns include name, category, rating, review count, opening hours signals, latitude/longitude, and geometry (\texttt{POINT}).
    \item[\texttt{points\_of\_interest}] Foot-traffic attractors grouped into categories (banks, education, health, temples, other). Columns include POI name, category, rating, review count, latitude/longitude, and geometry (\texttt{POINT}).
    \item[\texttt{demographics}] Neighborhood-level demographic proxies (e.g., zone polygons, average rent per square foot, population density estimates). Columns include zone identifier, polygon geometry (\texttt{POLYGON}), and derived numeric proxies.
\end{description}

\subsection{Road-Graph Storage}
The OSMnx road network graph for the study area (Kathmandu valley) is exported offline and serialized as \texttt{road\_graph.pkl} using Python's \texttt{pickle} module. The backend loads this file once at startup and retains it in memory. At inference time, shortest-path distances are computed on this in-memory graph rather than making live API calls, ensuring low-latency road-aware distance computation for both graph construction and baseline feature engineering.

\section{Feature Engineering and Data Structuring}

After scraping and cleaning, the project converts raw cafe/POI layers into \textbf{node-feature tensors and edge-index representations} suitable for the HeteroGNN. Feature generation is implemented using Python tooling (\textbf{pandas}, \textbf{NumPy}, \textbf{OSMnx}) plus the project's road-network utilities (used to compute shortest-path distances via the serialized road graph). The same feature logic is reused for scoring \textbf{user-selected coordinates} at inference time.

\subsubsection{Node Feature Engineering}
For the Heterogeneous Graph, each node type possesses a tailored feature vector constructed from the scraped metadata:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cafe / Candidate Nodes:} Features include intrinsic properties such as normalized star rating, log-scaled review count, NLP-derived sentiment scores, and price tier indicators. For training nodes (existing cafes), the ground truth \texttt{composite\_score} is attached as the regression target.
    \item \textbf{POI Nodes:} Categorical properties (e.g., banks, education, health) are one-hot encoded. Additional metadata such as rating and review count are included where available, allowing the model to distinguish between high-value POIs and minor ones. Missing values are imputed or handled via learnable embeddings.
    \item \textbf{Coordinates:} Latitude and longitude (\texttt{lat}, \texttt{lng}) are projected into a consistent CRS and optionally encoded via sinusoidal positional embeddings.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Edge Feature Engineering via OSMnx}
Instead of relying on static distance-decay tabular features, spatial relationships are modeled dynamically as typed edges (\texttt{cafe-near-poi}, \texttt{candidate-near-competitor}, etc.):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Road-Network Distance:} Distances are computed using the serialized OSMnx road graph (\texttt{road\_graph.pkl}), which is loaded into memory at backend startup. For any pair of nodes within the analysis radius, the shortest-path routing distance is calculated. If a valid road path cannot be found, the pipeline falls back to straight-line (Haversine) distance.
    \item \textbf{Edge Attributes:} The computed continuous distance (in kilometers) is attached to the corresponding edge as a quantitative attribute, allowing the HeteroGNN model's attention mechanism to naturally learn spatial decay patterns during message passing.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data Structuring and Outputs}
The engineered data is stored and consumed in the following way:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SpatiaLite database:} the primary runtime store for all spatial layers (listings, competitors, POIs, demographics). Queries use spatial indexes for fast neighborhood retrieval during both graph construction and map rendering.
    \item \textbf{Road-graph pickle (\texttt{road\_graph.pkl}):} the serialized OSMnx graph loaded at startup, used by the backend to compute road-network distances and provide road-topology edge features for the GNN.
    \item \textbf{HeteroData graph objects:} PyTorch Geometric \texttt{HeteroData} instances assembled per inference request, containing typed node feature tensors, edge indices, edge types, and optional edge attributes.
    \item \textbf{Baseline CSV feature tables (retained for ablation):} the legacy master CSV exports are retained for XGBoost baseline experiments and reproducibility checks, but are not used in the production inference pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data Cleaning (as implemented)}
The preprocessing code performs practical cleaning steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coordinate validation:} latitude/longitude columns are auto-detected, coerced to numeric, and non-finite coordinates are excluded before database insertion or graph construction.
    \item \textbf{Missing metadata handling:} when a POI/cafe weight field is absent, a safe fallback is used (proximity-based decay), ensuring feature generation does not fail due to incomplete scraped attributes.
    \item \textbf{Schema versioning:} graph artifact schemas (node types, edge types, feature dimensions) are versioned so that deployed model checkpoints remain compatible with graph construction outputs.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Dataset Partitioning (training pipeline)}
For model training and evaluation, the processed cafe node set is partitioned into train/validation/test subsets. Both random and spatial holdout splits are used: random splits for standard evaluation, and spatial block splits to test geographic generalization. Inference-time scoring reuses the same feature schema so predicted scores remain comparable to the trained target scale.

\section{Model Selection and Training}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Baseline Model (Linear Regression):} A linear regression model with standardized features serves as the baseline, providing a performance benchmark. The model is implemented using scikit-learn's \texttt{Pipeline} combining \texttt{StandardScaler} and \texttt{LinearRegression} to ensure proper feature normalization.
	
	\item \textbf{Baseline Comparator (XGBoost):} \texttt{xgb.XGBRegressor} is retained as a strong tabular baseline comparator for ablation and benchmarking.

	\item \textbf{Production Model (HeteroGNN with SAGEConv):} The deployed model is a heterogeneous graph neural network implemented using PyTorch Geometric's \texttt{HeteroConv} wrapper, with \textbf{SAGEConv} (GraphSAGE-style mean aggregation) for each relation type. This lightweight formulation delivers strong node-regression performance without the quadratic attention overhead of full HGT-style models.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Supports \textbf{heterogeneous graph semantics} with node types \texttt{place}, \texttt{category}, and \texttt{road\_node}, and relation types covering place-category, place-road, and road-road connections.
        \item Learns \textbf{relation-specific aggregation} via separate \texttt{SAGEConv} layers per edge type, projecting neighborhood mean embeddings through trainable weight matrices.
        \item Captures non-Euclidean spatial dependencies through multi-hop message passing across place--road--place and place--category--place paths.
        \item Input projections: \texttt{place} and \texttt{road\_node} features are linearly projected to the hidden dimension; \texttt{category} nodes use a learnable \texttt{Embedding} layer (vocabulary size 4000).
        \item Produces node-level regression outputs via a dedicated MLP head with Sigmoid activation, outputting a value in [0,1] that is multiplied by 100 to yield the final suitability score.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Training Protocol (HeteroGNN)}
The HeteroGNN training pipeline uses a \textbf{full-batch} graph training regime (the entire pre-built heterogeneous graph is processed in each forward pass):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full-batch training:} The complete \texttt{HeteroData} graph is loaded onto device and all place nodes are processed in a single forward pass per epoch, leveraging train/validation/test masks.
    \item \textbf{Loss function:} Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and target suitability scores (targets normalized to $[0,1]$; output multiplied by 100 for human-readable scale).
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} \texttt{torch.optim.Adam} with weight decay $10^{-4}$, initial learning rate $10^{-3}$.
    \item \textbf{Learning-rate scheduler:} \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} with patience of 10 epochs and reduction factor 0.5, monitoring validation MSE.
    \item \textbf{Gradient clipping:} Global gradient norm clipped to 1.0 per step to stabilise training.
    \item \textbf{Regularisation:} Batch normalisation on place embeddings and dropout ($p=0.2$) applied before the regression head.
    \item \textbf{Data splits:} 80\% train / 10\% validation / 10\% test, sampled via random permutation with fixed seed (42).
    \item \textbf{Ablation protocol:} Compare HeteroGNN against Linear Regression and XGBoost baselines under matched train/validation/test splits.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Model performance is assessed using both random holdout and spatially aware validation protocols:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} Measures the average absolute difference between predicted and actual scores, providing an interpretable error metric in the same units as the target variable.
	\item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):} Similar to MAE but penalizes larger errors more heavily, ensuring the model performs well across the entire distribution.
	\item \textbf{R-squared (R²):} Indicates the proportion of variance in the success score that is predictable from the input features, with values closer to 1.0 indicating better model fit.
	\item \textbf{Mean and Standard Deviation of Residuals:} Assess model bias and prediction consistency.
	\item \textbf{Spatial holdout / block cross-validation:} Tests geographic generalization by separating train/test regions spatially, reducing optimism from random neighborhood leakage.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Model Persistence and Deployment}
\begin{itemize}
	\item The production HeteroGNN model is persisted as a pure PyTorch state-dict checkpoint: \texttt{best\_hetero\_gnn.pth}. At inference time the backend re-instantiates the \texttt{HeteroGNN} class and loads the state dict directly (both legacy combined-checkpoint and new pure state-dict formats are supported).
	\item The pre-built heterogeneous graph (\texttt{hetero\_graph.pt}) and node/category mappings (\texttt{hetero\_graph\_mappings.pt}) are loaded once at service startup and kept in memory for fast inference.
	\item A \texttt{cKDTree} is built over the road-node coordinates at startup, enabling sub-millisecond nearest-road-node lookups during dynamic subgraph injection.
	\item Baseline artifacts are retained for evaluation reproducibility (e.g., \texttt{xgb\_baseline.pkl}, feature manifests, and split metadata).
	\item Runtime inference temporarily injects the selected location as a new \texttt{place} node, connects it to nearest road nodes and nearby category nodes, runs forward pass, reads the last-row prediction, then restores the graph to its original state (stateless inference).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Risk Assessment Framework}
The system translates the 0--100 suitability score into actionable risk categories. Two risk assessment layers exist:

\textbf{Raw GNN score (before contextualisation):}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{High Risk:} Score $< 40$ (location not recommended)
	\item \textbf{Medium Risk:} Score between 40 and 70 (proceed with caution)
	\item \textbf{Low Risk:} Score $\geq 70$ (favourable location)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contextualized score (after Requirements Profile modifiers):}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Low Risk:} Contextualized score $\geq 70$
	\item \textbf{Medium Risk:} Contextualized score between 40 and 70
	\item \textbf{High Risk:} Contextualized score $< 40$
\end{itemize}

\section{Real-Time Location Scoring System}

The core of the application is a system that provides data-driven suitability predictions for any location in the study area. The prediction workflow operates as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The user interacts with an \textbf{interactive map interface} displaying the study area.
	\item Upon selecting any point on the map, the frontend sends the geographic coordinates (latitude, longitude) to the backend API.
	\item The backend temporarily injects the query point as a new \texttt{place} node into the pre-built \texttt{HeteroData} graph. It connects the new node to the nearest \texttt{road\_node} entries (via \texttt{cKDTree}, within 0.005\textdegree) and inherits \texttt{category} edges from the nearest existing places:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Node features by type (\texttt{place}: lat, lng, open\_hrs, foot\_traffic; \texttt{road\_node}: lat, lng, street\_count; \texttt{category}: embedding index)
		\item Edge indices for all six typed relations
		\item Graph is restored to original state after inference (stateless)
	\end{itemize}
	\item The modified global graph is passed to the pre-trained HeteroGNN (SAGEConv) model for a single forward pass.
	\item The model returns a \textbf{Location Suitability Score} clamped to the range 0--100.
	\item The inference pipeline is optimized to handle concurrent requests efficiently.
	\item The system categorizes scores into risk levels: High Risk ($<$40), Medium Risk (40--70), Low Risk ($\geq$70).
\end{itemize}



\subsubsection{Actual Subgraph Input Structure}
At inference time the \texttt{GNNPredictionService} temporarily injects the query location as a new \texttt{place} node into the pre-built global \texttt{HeteroData} object. The effective input structure is:

\section{Business Requirements Profile}
Before any spatial analysis is performed, the user completes a multi-step \textbf{Business Requirements Profile Form} that captures the full business context required for personalized scoring. The form collects the following fields:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cafe Type:} Category of the business (e.g., specialty coffee, bakery-cafe, study cafe).
    \item \textbf{Business Scale:} Expected venue size---\texttt{small}, \texttt{medium}, or \texttt{large}.
    \item \textbf{Pricing Tier:} Positioning---\texttt{budget}, \texttt{mid-range}, or \texttt{premium}.
    \item \textbf{Primary Audience:} Target customer segments (\texttt{students}, \texttt{professionals}, \texttt{families}, \texttt{tourists}).
    \item \textbf{Peak Hours:} Expected busy periods (\texttt{morning}, \texttt{afternoon}, \texttt{evening}).
    \item \textbf{Seating Preference:} Service model---\texttt{dine-in}, \texttt{takeaway}, or \texttt{balanced}.
    \item \textbf{Competitor Avoidance:} Whether to penalise locations within a user-defined minimum competitor distance (in metres).
    \item \textbf{Business Stage:} Operational maturity---\texttt{new}, \texttt{existing}, or \texttt{expanding}.
\end{itemize}

These parameters are submitted together with the selected location coordinates to the \texttt{/api/v1/analysis/wizard-report/} endpoint, which triggers the full contextualized analysis pipeline.

\section{Contextualized Suitability Scoring}
The final suitability value used in the production application is no longer based solely on the raw graph-model output. Instead, the system computes a \textbf{contextualized score} (on a 0--100 scale) that combines five complementary signals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GNN Location Score:} The core suitability signal produced by the HeteroGNN model from the dynamically constructed local heterogeneous subgraph. Normalized to 0--100 and clamped before further adjustment.
    \item \textbf{POI-Fit Score:} A general measure of how well the surrounding area is served by amenities. Computed as $\min(\text{total POIs within 1 km} / 50,\; 1.0) \times 100$.
    \item \textbf{Audience-Fit Score:} A business-context adjustment derived from a weighted sum of POI-category counts matched against the target audience profile. Each audience type (students, professionals, families, tourists) has a predefined POI-category weight table; 5 POIs per category yields a ``full'' category score.
    \item \textbf{Road Accessibility Score:} A network-aware accessibility component fetched from the road-types summary endpoint, reflecting the quality and connectivity of the road network at the selected location.
    \item \textbf{Competition Penalty:} A cumulative deduction (capped at 30 points) applied when the pricing tier is mismatched with local cafe density, when competitor-avoidance is breached, or when the venue scale is inconsistent with foot-traffic density.
\end{itemize}

The final contextualized score is:
\begin{align}
\text{Score}_{\text{ctx}} = \text{clamp}\bigl(&\text{GNN Score} + \Delta_{\text{audience}} + \Delta_{\text{pricing}} \nonumber\\
&+ \Delta_{\text{seating}} + \Delta_{\text{scale}} + \Delta_{\text{stage}} - \text{Competition Penalty},\; 0,\; 100\bigr)
\end{align}

The system also generates human-readable \textbf{strengths}, \textbf{risks}, and \textbf{context insights} based on each modifier's direction and magnitude, enabling the user to understand exactly why a location scored as it did.
